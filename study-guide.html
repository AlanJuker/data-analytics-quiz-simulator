1. The midterm at a glance

The midterm has four questions based on applied scenarios from Modules 1–4. You will be given analytical information (a data extract, summary statistics or test output) and asked to interpret it and make evidence-based decisions. Marks come from applying concepts to the case, not from reciting definitions or copying numbers.

2. Answer framework for scenario questions

Use the same six moves for every scenario. Not every part asks for all six, but thinking through them stops you from missing marks.

  1. Frame. Restate the decision and the variables. Which is the independent variable (IV) and which is the dependent variable (DV)? Is it a population or a sample?
  2. Check the data. Look for quality problems in the exhibit: duplicates, impossible values, inconsistent codes or formats, missing values (and their type), outliers, outdated or irrelevant data, sampling bias.
  3. Classify. Give the measurement level of each variable involved, because it decides what you may calculate.
  4. Choose and justify. Name the summary, chart or test, then say why it fits (variable levels, question asked) and why the obvious alternative does not.
  5. Check assumptions. List the test's assumptions, compare them with the case, and switch method or fix the data if one fails.
  6. Interpret and conclude. Report direction, strength (the coefficient) and significance (p) separately. Use association language, name a confounder or limitation, and recommend a next step.

Worked mini-example

A gym surveyed 60 members: hours of exercise per week and stress rating
(1 = very low ... 5 = very high). Spearman ρ = −.41, p = .001, n = 60.

This full-mark style answer covers all six moves.

Exercise hours are ratio and the stress rating is ordinal, so Pearson's r is not appropriate. Spearman's ρ fits because it uses ranks, measures monotonic relationships and needs no normality. ρ = −.41 shows a moderate, negative relationship: members who exercise more tend to report lower stress. With p = .001 (< .05), the result is statistically significant, so it is unlikely to be due to chance alone. The data are correlational, so they do not show that exercise reduces stress: members with less demanding jobs may have both more free time to exercise and lower stress. A study that randomly assigns an exercise program would be needed to test cause.

3. Module 1 · Introduction to data analytics

Core ideas

  • Data analysis examines, cleans, transforms and interprets raw data to find patterns, trends and relationships that support decisions.
  • Data lifecycle: research question & hypothesis → data generation/collection → storage → processing (cleaning, normalization, transformation) → analysis → visualization & interpretation (→ decision-making).
  • Five benefits: informed decision-making, predictive capabilities, enhanced performance and efficiency, personalization and customer service, risk management.
  • Cleaning functions: remove irrelevant data, correct errors, handle missing data, standardize formats, identify and remove duplicates. Tools: Excel/Google Sheets, OpenRefine, pandas (Python), R.

Hypotheses

  • A hypothesis is a testable statement about the relationship between variables. It guides which variables to collect and which method to use.
  • Steps: clear research question → define the IV and DV → explore data or literature → write an "if…then" hypothesis → make it specific, measurable and falsifiable → align it with the business goal.
  • H₀ (null): no difference or relationship. H₁ (alternative): there is one (directional only if predicted in advance).

In a scenario: you may be asked to place a step in the lifecycle (cleaning is processing), to identify the IV and DV, or to write H₀ and H₁ for the case.

4. Module 2 · Data collection & preparation

Sources and types

  • Primary data are collected by you for this question (surveys, interviews, experiments): tailored, but slower and costlier. Secondary data were collected for another purpose (reports, government statistics, company systems): quick and cheap, but they may not fit the question or be timely.
  • Quantitative data are numeric and measurable (test hypotheses, generalize). Qualitative data are non-numeric (text, images) and explain the "why" and "how".
  • Data structures: time-series (one entity over time), cross-sectional (many entities at one time, a snapshot), panel (many entities over time, which helps study change and causal questions).

Variable roles

VariableMeaning
Independent (IV)The factor manipulated or believed to influence the outcome
Dependent (DV)The outcome measured
ControlledKept constant so it cannot influence the result
ExtraneousNot of interest, but could influence the result if not controlled (a source of confounding)
ModeratingChanges the strength or direction of the IV → DV relationship
MediatingExplains how the IV affects the DV (the mechanism in between)

Preparing data

  • Duplicates: identical or near-identical records. Remove or merge them, or they skew results.
  • Inconsistencies: the same entity coded differently (F / Female / f, mixed date formats). Standardize them.
  • Encoding categories: label encoding gives integers but can imply a false order for nominal data. One-hot encoding (0/1 columns) suits nominal data. Ordinal encoding keeps a natural order. Binary encoding and frequency encoding are further options.
  • Scaling: min–max normalization (0–1) is sensitive to outliers. Z-score standardization (mean 0, SD 1) keeps the shape and suits comparing relative positions.
  • Outliers and missing values: see the quick reference in §7.
  • Sampling: a sample should be representative. Random or stratified sampling reduces bias; surveying only easy-to-reach or active people creates sampling (selection) bias.

5. Module 3 · Statistical measurement & visualization

Describing vs generalizing

  • Descriptive statistics summarize the data you have (mean, median, mode, range, variance, SD, charts). Inferential statistics use a sample to draw conclusions about a population (estimation, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, regression).
  • Population = the whole group of interest. Sample = the subset studied, which should be representative.

Center, spread, shape

  • Mean uses every value but is pulled by outliers. Median is the middle value and is robust to outliers. Mode is the most frequent value and the only measure of center for nominal data.
  • Range = max − min, the quickest but crudest measure of spread. Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean. SD = √variance, in the data's own units. IQR = Q3 − Q1, the middle 50%, robust to outliers.
  • Right-skewed (long tail to the right): mean > median > mode, as with income or delivery times. Left-skewed: mean < median < mode. For skewed data report the median and IQR.
  • Boxplot = minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum. Whiskers reach 1.5 × IQR; points beyond them are outliers. A median off-center in the box, or unequal whiskers, signals skew.

Choosing and judging charts

PurposeChart
Compare categoriesBar chart (clustered or 100% stacked to compare groups of different sizes)

Do: choose the right chart, keep it simple, label axes clearly, use color for meaning (colorblind-safe), stay consistent, highlight the key point, and use honest scales. Don't: truncate axes to exaggerate differences, overuse color, clutter, ignore the audience, use the wrong chart type, or skip data checks. Visualization serves four purposes: idea generation, idea illustration, visual discovery and everyday data visualization.

6. Module 4 · Measures of association

  • Association means that changes in one variable correspond to changes in another. It does not imply causation.
  • Ways to measure it: a correlation coefficient (−1 to +1: strength and direction), covariance (direction only, not standardized), and a contingency table with a chi-square test (categorical variables).
  • Parametric tests (Pearson, t-test, ANOVA) assume interval or ratio data and a known, usually normal, distribution. They are more powerful when the assumptions hold. Non-parametric tests (Spearman, Kendall, chi-square) suit ordinal data, non-normal data, small samples or outliers.
  • Pearson's r measures a linear relationship between two continuous variables. Assumptions: linearity (check a scatterplot), continuous interval or ratio data, no extreme outliers (r is very sensitive to them), and approximate normality for the significance test (less critical when n > 30).
  • Spearman's ρ measures a monotonic relationship with ordinal data or non-normal continuous data. It needs no normality and is robust to outliers.
  • Kendall's τ measures a monotonic relationship between ranked variables. It is preferred for small samples or many tied ranks, and it is more conservative.
  • Chi-square test of independence tests whether two categorical variables are associated. It compares observed counts with the counts expected if the variables were independent (E = row total × column total / grand total). Assumptions: categorical count data, independent observations (each case in one cell), expected count ≥ 5 in at least 80% of cells. Otherwise combine categories, or use Fisher's exact test for 2×2 tables.
  • Cramér's V measures the strength of a chi-square association: 0 = none, 1 = perfect.
  • One- vs two-tailed: two-tailed is the default. Use one-tailed only if the direction was predicted before data collection; switching after seeing the results is p-hacking and inflates Type I error.
  • SPSS: Analyze → Correlate → Bivariate (Pearson, Spearman, Kendall). Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Crosstabs (chi-square, Cramér's V).

ReliabilityIs the source trustworthy?Unknown source; self-selected respondents

Missing values

TypeMissingness depends on…Treatment
MCARNothing (purely random)Listwise or pairwise deletion, mean or median imputation
MAROther observed variables (e.g., age)Multiple imputation or regression imputation
MNARThe missing value itself, or something unobserved (e.g., disengaged people skip the engagement survey)Imputation is risky; use complex modelling, report the limitation, run a sensitivity analysis

Little's MCAR test: H₀ = data are MCAR. If p ≤ .05, reject it and use model-based methods. Mean imputation underestimates variance; flagging (an indicator variable) records which values were imputed.

Outliers

  • Detect: IQR rule (below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR), z-score beyond ±3, boxplot dots, histogram gaps, distant scatterplot points.
  • Decide first: is it an error (correct or remove it), a different population, or a rare but real event (keep it)?
  • Treat real outliers: log or √x transformation for right-skewed data with valid large values; winsorization for a few extreme values that are not analytically important; reciprocal (1/x) for highly skewed timing data. Or report robust statistics (median, IQR, Spearman).

Trap: a variable coded with numbers is not automatically interval or ratio. A 1–5 rating is ordinal, and a zip code is nominal. Ratio allows "twice as much" statements; interval does not (20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C).

10. Statistical significance vs strength

  • Significance (p-value) answers "Is the relationship likely to be real rather than chance?" If p < .05, reject H₀ (no relationship). The p-value is evidence against H₀, not a measure of size or importance.
  • Strength (the coefficient) answers "How strong is it?" For r, ρ and τ, use |r| ≈ .30 for weak, ≈ .50 for moderate and ≥ .70 for strong. The sign gives the direction. Cramér's V runs from 0 (none) to 1 (perfect).
  • Large samples make tiny effects significant; small samples can leave moderate effects non-significant. Always report both.
Strong / moderate coefficientWeak coefficient
p < .05Real and meaningful. e.g., r = .62, p < .001 → "a strong, significant positive relationship"Real but small. e.g., r = .09, p < .001, n = 2,000 → "significant but very weak, little practical importance"
p ≥ .05Possibly real, not proven. e.g., r = .52, p = .08, n = 12 → "moderate but not significant; the sample is too small to rule out chance"No evidence. e.g., V = .07, p = .84 → "no evidence of an association (which is not proof of none)"

11. Association vs causation

A significant correlation or chi-square shows that variables move together. It does not show that one causes the other. Common reasons:

  • Confounding (extraneous) variable: a third factor drives both. For example, shift type affects both sleep and sick days, and store size affects both ad budget and sales.
  • Reverse causation: the outcome may drive the "cause". High-selling stores may receive bigger ad budgets.
  • Selection effects: people choose their own group. Motivated employees both take more training and perform better.
  • Chance: especially with many tests or small samples.

Stronger evidence for cause comes from a randomized controlled experiment or A/B test, a clear time order (cause before effect), and controlling for confounders (comparing within groups). Panel data that follow the same units over time also help.

SayDon't say
"X is associated with Y""X causes / drives / leads to Y"
"Customers who … tend to …""Increasing X will increase Y"
"This may reflect [confounder]; an experiment would be needed to test cause""This proves…"

12. Communicating conclusions

Sentence templates

Correlation: "There is a [weak / moderate / strong], [positive / negative] [linear / monotonic] relationship between X and Y (r / ρ / τ = __, p = __, n = __). [It is / It is not] statistically significant at .05. Because the data are correlational, this does not show that X causes Y; [confounder] may explain part of it."
Chi-square: "There [is / is no] statistically significant association between A and B (χ²(df) = __, p = __). Cramér's V = __ indicates a [weak / moderate / strong] association."
Recommendation: "Based on this evidence, we recommend [action]. Before [big decision], [validate with an experiment / collect more recent data / fix the data issue]."

What a full-mark answer contains

  • The specific numbers from the exhibit, not generic theory
  • Direction, strength and significance, each stated separately
  • A justification for every choice (why this, why not the alternative)
  • At least one assumption or limitation tied to the case
  • Association wording and a plausible confounder
  • A clear, actionable conclusion in plain language

Common mistakes

  • Calling a significant result "strong", or a non-significant one "proof of no relationship"
  • Using a mean or Pearson's r for ordinal ratings
  • Running Pearson with a clear outlier or a curved scatterplot
  • Deleting outliers without checking whether they are errors
  • Mean-imputing data that are MAR or MNAR
  • Ignoring the chi-square expected-count rule
  • Switching to one-tailed after seeing the results
  • Generalizing from a biased or non-random sample

13. Final review checklist

  • I can name the six data-quality dimensions and spot each one in a table.
  • I can tell MCAR, MAR and MNAR apart from a description and give the right treatment.
  • I can apply the IQR rule and explain how to treat a real outlier.
  • I can classify any variable as nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio and say what that allows.
  • I can choose the mean or median from the shape of the data and justify it.
  • I can pick the right chart and spot a misleading one.
  • I can choose Pearson, Spearman, Kendall or chi-square, and list each one's assumptions.
  • I can interpret r / ρ / τ / V with p, keeping strength and significance separate.
  • I can explain why an association is not causation and name a confounder.
  • I can write a two-sentence conclusion with numbers, a limitation and a next step.
