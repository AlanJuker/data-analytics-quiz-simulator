Exam technique

How to answer

Find the design first (how many groups? independent or the same people?), then read any output in the same order every time.

25 ×Multiple choice: four moves

  1. Find the designOne sample against a benchmark? Two groups? The same people twice? Three or more groups? Two factors? That decides the test (see the test chooser).
  2. Check the dataSkewed, small sample, outliers or ordinal ratings (1–5) → think non-parametric.
  3. Read an output in this orderLevene's test → p-value vs .05 → direction (means or mean ranks) → next step (post hoc?).
  4. Distrust absolute words"Proves", "accept H₀" and "all groups differ" are almost always wrong.

2 ×Short answer: three sentences

① Answer

Name the test, the distribution or the decision.

② Why

Give the condition, definition or numbers that justify it.

③ So what

Module 5

Probability for decision making

A probability measures how likely an event is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Which distribution you use depends on what you count or measure.

5.1Probability basics

0impossible0.5as likely as not1certain

To compute a probability, first find the total number of possible outcomes, then count how many of them make up the event.

The Last BananaTwo dice are rolled and only the highest number counts. Player 1 wins with 1–4, Player 2 with 5–6. Player 1 needs both dice to show 1–4 (16 of 36 outcomes), while Player 2 needs only one high die (20 of 36). Player 2 is favoured.

5.2Which distribution?

Binomial

"How many out of n?"

Countssuccesses in a fixed number of trials (0 to n) Parametersn (trials) and p (chance of success) Conditionsfixed n2 outcomesconstant pindependent ExampleHow many of 20 customers click an ad Centremean = n × p, SD = √(np(1 − p))

Poisson

"How many per hour / per page / per km?"

Countsevents in a fixed interval of time or space, with no upper limit Parameterλ (lambda) = average number per interval Conditionscountsindependentconstant rateno two at once ExampleCalls per hour, arrivals per night, defects per batch Centremean = λ. Rescale λ to the interval: 30 per hour = 5 per 10 minutes

Normal

"How long, how heavy, how much?"

Describesa continuous measurement that clusters around the mean Parametersμ (mean) and σ (standard deviation) Shapesymmetric bellmean = median = modetails never touch the axis ExampleHeights, delivery times, sample means

5.3The normal curve and the 68–95–99.7 rule

μ moves the centre

A larger mean slides the curve right; the shape stays the same.

σ sets the width

A larger SD makes the curve wider and flatter.

ExampleDelivery times have mean 30 min and SD 5 min. About 68% take 25–35 min, 95% take 20–40 min and 99.7% take 15–45 min.

5.4z-scores and the Z-table

z = (X − μ) ÷ σhow many standard deviations a value is from the mean · negative = below the mean
Why z-scores helpAna scored 80 (class mean 70, SD 5) → z = 2. Ben scored 85 (class mean 80, SD 10) → z = 0.5. Ana did better relative to her class, even with the lower raw score.

The Z-table gives the area to the LEFT of z

Less than z

Use the table value.

Greater than z

1 − table value.

Between z₁ and z₂

Larger value − smaller value.

Example: P(Z < 1) = .8413, so P(Z > 1) = 1 − .8413 = .1587. The Z-table is used for confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

5.5Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

As the sample size grows, the distribution of sample means becomes approximately normal, whatever the shape of the population.

CentreMean of the sample means = μ
Spread (standard error)σ ÷ √n: 4 × the sample = half the error
Why it mattersNormal-based tests work on large samples (n > 30)

LimitThe CLT does not rescue small samples (e.g. n = 8) from a skewed population.

5.6When the normal distribution fails, and the fix

LimitationExampleWhat to do
Assumes symmetryIncomes, spending (mean ≫ median)Log or square-root transformation
Needs continuous dataCounts of defects or callsPoisson or binomial instead
Sensitive to outliersA few huge purchasesTrim or treat outliers, or go non-parametric
Underestimates extreme eventsMarket crashes, insurance claimsAnother distribution (log-normal, exponential, Weibull)
Data have a finite rangePercentages from 0 to 100Transformation or a bounded model
CLT misappliedn = 8 from a skewed populationLarger sample, or a non-parametric method

Module 6

Hypothesis testing

A test asks: if there were really no effect (H₀), how surprising would these data be? A small p-value means very surprising, so we reject H₀.

6.1H₀ and Ha

H₀ · null hypothesis

No difference, no relationship, no effect. The baseline the results are measured against.

μ₁ = μ₂difference = 0

Ha · alternative hypothesis

There is a difference or relationship that is not due to chance. What the researcher hopes to support.

≠<>

The only two decisions

Reject H₀ (p ≤ α)

The data give evidence for Ha. It is not proof.

Fail to reject H₀ (p > α)

Not enough evidence. It does not prove H₀ is true.

Never write"We accept H₀" or "this proves Ha".

6.2One tail or two? Decide before collecting data

A drug company claims a 20% gain in recovery. What does the researcher suspect?

Two-tailed · Ha: μ ≠ 20%

"It is different, in either direction." α is split: 2.5% in each tail. Critical z = ±1.96.

Left-tailed · Ha: μ < 20%

"It is lower than claimed." All 5% in the left tail. Critical z = −1.645.

Right-tailed · Ha: μ > 20%

"It is higher than claimed." All 5% in the right tail. Critical z = +1.645.

RuleA direction predicted before seeing the data → one-tailed. "Is it different?" → two-tailed. In a t-table, choose the column for one- or two-tailed α and the row for the degrees of freedom.

6.3Making the decision

p-value approach

p = the probability of results at least this extreme if H₀ were true.

p ≤ α (usually .05) → reject H₀. It also shows how strong the evidence is: the smaller p, the stronger.

Critical value approach

Find the cut-off for α, then compute the test statistic.

Statistic beyond the critical value → reject H₀. It only says inside or outside the rejection region.

Test statistic (z, t, F)One number calculated from the sample.
p-valueA probability calculated from that statistic.

Both approaches usually reach the same decision.

6.4z-test or t-test?

z-test

  • Population σ known
  • or a large sample (n > 30)
  • Uses the standard normal distribution

t-test

  • σ unknown, estimated from the sample
  • Small sample (n < 30)
  • t-distribution: heavier tails, shaped by the degrees of freedom

With large samples the t-distribution is practically normal, so both tests give very similar results. Software usually runs a t-test.

6.5Errors and power

Decision
H₀ is true
H₀ is false
Reject H₀
Type I errorfalse positive · probability α
Correctpower = 1 − β
Fail to reject H₀

Type II errorfalse negative · probability β
Type I exampleConcluding a useless drug works.
Type II exampleConcluding an effective drug does not work.

Raise α (.05 → .10)

  • ↑ easier to reject H₀
  • ↑ Type I errors (false alarms)
  • ↓ Type II errors
  • ↑ power

Lowering α does the opposite.

More power, same α

Increase the sample size (or the effect size). Real effects become easier to detect without more false positives.

Security guardA guard who is quick to press the alarm (high α) catches more burglars (more power) but is also fooled by more raccoons (more Type I errors).

6.6The three t-tests (class activity)

One-sample

sample mean ⟷ a known value

ActivityIs average salary different from the $75,000 benchmark?H₀μ = 75,000

Independent samples

group A | group B (different people)

ActivityDoes average salary differ between men and women?H₀μ₁ = μ₂ · Levene's test picks the row

Paired samples

before → after (the same people)

ActivityDid each employee's sales change after training? (matched by EmployeeID)H₀mean difference = 0

Independent = separate populations, or one group randomly split in two; one sample does not influence the other. Paired = each value is linked to one value in the other set.

Directional versionIf it was predicted in advance that men earn more: Ha: μmen > μwomen (one-tailed). Same for "sales increased after training".

6.7Reading an independent t-test

Levene's test
p > .05 → variances are equal → read the "equal variances assumed" row
p ≤ .05 → variances differ → read the "equal variances not assumed" row (Welch's t-test)
  • Levene's p = .003 ≤ .05 → the variances differ → use the "not assumed" row.
  • p = .032 ≤ .05 → reject H₀: delivery time differs by region, by about 4.1 minutes.
  • Levene's p is about variances only. It picks the row; it is not the answer.
95% CI of the difference excludes 0Significant difference
95% CI includes 0Not significant

Paired test on training"Sales rose significantly (p = .004)" is right. "The training caused it" is not: without a control group, other factors (season, market) could explain the rise.

6.8Steps of a test and the report

  1. Set upDefine H₀ and Ha → select the test → choose α (.05, .01 or .10).
  2. RunCollect the data → compute the test statistic → find the p-value.
  3. Decide and reportp ≤ α → reject H₀. Report the test statistic, the p-value and the decision, then a plain-language conclusion.
t(24) = 3.18, p = .004 → sales rose significantly after training (mean +$1,240).A complete one-line report

Sampling strategies mentioned in this module: simple random, stratified (random within each subgroup) and cluster sampling.

Module 7

ANOVA (analysis of variance)

ANOVA tests whether three or more group means differ by comparing the spread between the groups with the spread inside them.

7.1Why ANOVA instead of several t-tests

3 groups → 3 t-tests

Each test has a 5% false-positive risk, so the risks add up: the Type I error rate inflates.

3 groups → 1 ANOVA

One test compares all the means at once and keeps the error rate at α.

H₀All group means are equal
H₁At least one mean differs
VariablesCategorical factor (the groups) + continuous outcome

7.2The F-ratio

F = between-group variance ÷ within-group variance
Between-group varianceHow far the group means are from the overall mean: the effect of the factor.
Within-group (error) varianceThe spread of scores inside each group, which the factor does not explain.

7.3Assumptions: check, and what to do if they fail

Independence

CheckStudy design (random assignment, each person measured once)If it failsFix the design

Normality of the residuals

residual = a score − its group mean

CheckQ-Q plot, Shapiro–Wilk, Kolmogorov–SmirnovIf it failsTransformation, or Kruskal–Wallis

Equal variances

CheckLevene's test (p > .05 = OK)If it failsWelch's ANOVA (the course's choice). Ignoring it → more Type I errors.

Random sampling

CheckHow the data were collectedIf it failsResults cannot be generalised

Two-way designsUnequal variances plus unequal group sizes can bias the results, and Welch's ANOVA is not available. Use a transformation, a generalised linear model or a non-parametric alternative.

7.4One-way vs two-way

One-way ANOVA

One factor with 3+ levels.

Flashcards vs summarising vs self-testing → exam score

Two-way ANOVA

Two factors → two main effects + one interaction.

Teaching method × classroom → test score

Main effectOne factor's own effect, averaged over the other.
InteractionThe effect of one factor depends on the level of the other.

MANOVA extends ANOVA to several dependent variables at once.

7.5After a significant F

  1. Significant F = at least one mean differsIt does not say which one.
  2. Post hoc test → which pairs differTukey HSD when variances are equal (the course mentions Bonferroni when they are not).
  3. Effect size η² (eta-squared)The share of the outcome's variance explained by the factor: η² = .17 → about 17%.

7.6Reading ANOVA outputs

  • Levene's p = .318 > .05 → equal variances, so the standard ANOVA is fine.
  • p = .005 → at least one region's mean differs.
  • Tukey: only A and C differ (p = .003); A vs B is not significant.
  • Both main effects are significant: method and study time each matter.
  • The interaction is significant: the method's effect depends on study time.

Module 8

Non-parametric tests

When the data are skewed, small, ordinal or have outliers, rank them and use the non-parametric version of the test.

8.1Why rank the data

Parametric (t-test, ANOVA)

  • Assumes a population shape, usually normal
  • Uses the raw values (means, variances)
  • More powerful when its assumptions hold

Non-parametric

  • No specific distribution; no normality needed
  • Uses ranks (or medians, signs)
  • Robust to skew, outliers, small samples, ordinal scales

Ranking tames an outlier

Value12151821950 Rank12345

950 would drag a mean far up, but as a rank it is just "5th". That is why rank-based tests are robust.

Use a non-parametric test when

data are skewedthe sample is smallthere are outliersthe scale is ordinal (1–5)t-test / ANOVA assumptions fail

8.2The four tests and what they replace

Independent t-test→Mann–Whitney U2 independent groups · H₀: the two distributions are the same · e.g. satisfaction online vs in-store
Paired t-test→Wilcoxon signed-rank2 related measurements · H₀: median difference = 0 · e.g. pain before vs after treatment
One-way ANOVA→Kruskal–Wallis3+ independent groups · H₀: all distributions are the same · e.g. satisfaction at 3 stores
Repeated-measures ANOVA→Friedman3+ related measurements · e.g. the same patients at 3 time points

All fourneed data that can be ranked (ordinal or continuous).

8.3How each one works

Mann–Whitney U

HowRanks all values from both groups together; does one group tend to have higher ranks?AssumesIndependent groups, ordinal/continuous data, similar shapes (only then is it a difference in medians), no major ties Not forPaired data (→ Wilcoxon) or 3+ groups (→ Kruskal–Wallis)

Wilcoxon signed-rank

HowTakes the absolute differences, ranks them, re-applies the + / − signs, tests whether the median difference is 0AssumesPairs, differences at least ordinal and roughly symmetric Choose it whenn < 30 with skewed differences, Likert data or extreme outliers. With n ≥ 30 or normal differences, use the paired t-test.

Kruskal–Wallis

HowRanks all observations across the groupsAssumesEach person in only one group; similar shapes (check with boxplots + Levene's test, skewness within ±1) RememberAlways non-directional. Similar shapes → medians; different shapes → distributions. Significant → post hoc pairwise tests.

Two factors, non-parametric

Scheirer–Ray–HareExtends Kruskal–Wallis to two-way designsART ANOVAAligned rank transform: the most accurate for testing interactions

8.4Reading non-parametric outputs

  • p = .025 < .05 → satisfaction differs between the store types.
  • Online has the higher mean rank → online customers tend to be more satisfied.
  • A negative Z does not make the test invalid; the p-value decides.
  • Mostly negative ranks and p = .008 → scores dropped significantly.
  • p = .019 → at least one branch differs; South ranks highest.
  • Only post hoc pairwise tests can say which branches differ.

All the tests

Every test, one by one

Every test asks the same thing: is the difference in this sample too big to be chance? The tests differ only in who is compared and what kind of data you have. Get those two right and the rest is reading p.

t-tests, ANOVAcompare means (ANOVA uses variances to do it, but tests means)
Mann–Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis, Friedmancompare ranks: roughly medians or whole distributions
Levene'scompares variances (spread), only to check an assumption
Shapiro–Wilkcompares the shape with a normal curve, only to check an assumption

T.1The 4-step routine for any test question

  1. Who is compared?One group against a number · two different groups · the same people twice · three or more groups · the same people three or more times · two factors at once.
  2. What kind of data?Normal, interval data → the parametric test (t-test, ANOVA). Skewed, ordinal (1–5 ratings), small sample or outliers → its non-parametric twin (works on ranks).
  3. Decide with pp ≤ .05 → reject H₀, the result is significant. p > .05 → fail to reject H₀, no significant difference. Compare the "2-tailed" p with .05 directly.
  4. Say which way, in the design's wordsLook at the means, mean ranks or +/− ranks for the direction. Different people → "the groups differ". Same people → "scores changed (rose / dropped)".

T.2Same people or different people?

This is the question most wrong answers get wrong. Look for these clue words.

Independent: different people

Each person is in one group only.

men vs womenonline vs in-storeNorth, South, West branchesthree teaching methodstreatment vs control group

TestsIndependent t-test · Mann–Whitney U · one-way ANOVA · Kruskal–Wallis

Related: the same people again

Each value is linked to a value in the other set.

before vs afterthe same patientseach employeematched by IDat 3 time points

TestsPaired t-test · Wilcoxon signed-rank · repeated-measures ANOVA · Friedman

Quick checkCan you draw a line from each value on one side to one value on the other side (the same person)? Yes → related. No → independent.

Every parametric test has a non-parametric twin for the same design. Learn them in pairs.

Why the twins existParametric tests use the raw values and assume normal data; they are more powerful when that holds. The twins rank the values first, so skew and outliers stop mattering. Same question, safer method.

T.4One or two samples

T.6Helper tests: they never answer the research question

These run before or after the main test. A common wrong answer reads their p as the result.

Levene's test

ChecksEqual variances across the groups p > .05Equal → standard t-test row / standard ANOVA p ≤ .05Unequal → Welch's t-test / Welch's ANOVA

Shapiro–Wilk

ChecksIs the data (or the residuals) normal? p > .05Normal enough → parametric test p ≤ .05Not normal → consider the non-parametric twin

Post hoc (Tukey HSD)

WhenOnly after a significant ANOVA (or Kruskal–Wallis, with pairwise tests) AnswersWhich pairs of groups differ

η² (eta-squared)

WhatEffect size: how big the effect is, not whether it is significant Readη² = .17 → the factor explains about 17% of the variance

Flip for the helpersFor Levene's and Shapiro–Wilk, p > .05 is the good news (the assumption holds). For the main test, p ≤ .05 is the finding.

T.7Which way did it go?

p only says whether there is a difference. The direction always comes from another part of the output:

t-testsThe group means or the mean difference (sign of t follows which group was subtracted)
Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis, FriedmanThe higher mean rank = higher values
WilcoxonMore negative ranks = second measurement lower; more positive = higher
ANOVAThe group means, and the post hoc test for which pairs
Negative Z or tOnly the direction. It does not make the result invalid or non-significant

Two-tailed (the default)

Ha: "there is a difference, either way". Compare the 2-tailed p with .05. You can still say which way it went: first p says there is a difference, then the means or ranks show the direction.

One-tailed

Ha names a direction before the data ("pain will drop"). One-tailed p = 2-tailed p ÷ 2, but only if the result went the predicted way. Matters near the cut-off: 2-tailed .08 → one-tailed .04.

T.8Say it right: test by test

Wilcoxon: "The two groups of patients differ"
"Pain dropped significantly after treatment" (one group, measured twice)
Mann–Whitney: "Satisfaction changed over time"
"Online customers were more satisfied than in-store customers" (two groups)
p > .05: "The groups are the same"
"No significant difference" (not enough evidence; equality is not proven)
ANOVA / Kruskal–Wallis: "All the branches differ" or "South differs from North"
"At least one branch differs"; only the post hoc test names the pairs
Levene's p = .003: "Delivery time differs by region"
"The variances differ, so read the Welch row", then use that row's p
Shapiro–Wilk p = .30: "Significant, reject H₀"
"The data look normal", so the parametric test is fine
Paired t-test: "The training caused the rise"
"Sales rose significantly"; without a control group other causes are possible
Two-way ANOVA interaction: "Both factors matter"
"The effect of method depends on study time" (that is the interaction; main effects are separate rows)
Two-tailed p = .03: "Compare with .025, so not significant"
Compare with .05 → significant (.025 per tail is only for critical values: ±1.96)

Decide

Test chooser

Two questions decide almost every test: how many groups (and are they different people or the same people)? Are the data normal and interval, or skewed, ordinal or small?

Module 5 · Probability

What are the four conditions of a binomial experiment?

A fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, a constant probability of success and independent trials.

How is Poisson different from binomial?

Binomial counts successes in a fixed number of trials (maximum n). Poisson counts events in a fixed interval of time or space, using the average rate λ, with no upper limit.

What does the Central Limit Theorem say, and why does it matter?

Sample means become approximately normal as n grows, whatever the population's shape, with spread σ/√n. It lets us use normal-based tests on large samples.

What does z = −1.5 mean?

The value is 1.5 standard deviations below the mean.

Module 6 · Hypothesis testing

What is a p-value, and when do you reject H₀?

The probability of results at least this extreme if H₀ were true. Reject H₀ when p ≤ α (usually .05).

Type I vs Type II error?

Type I: rejecting a true H₀ (false positive, probability α). Type II: failing to reject a false H₀ (false negative, probability β).

What happens when α goes from .05 to .10?

H₀ is rejected more easily: more Type I errors, fewer Type II errors, more power.

When do you use a z-test instead of a t-test?

When σ is known, or the sample is large (n > 30). Otherwise a t-test.

Levene's p = .006 in an independent t-test. Which row?

Equal variances not assumed (Welch's t-test), because p ≤ .05 means the variances differ.

Module 7 · ANOVA

Why ANOVA instead of several t-tests?

Several t-tests inflate the Type I error rate; one ANOVA compares all the means in a single test at α.

What does the F-ratio compare?

Between-group variance to within-group (error) variance. A large F means the means differ more than chance explains.

What is an interaction effect?

The effect of one factor depends on the level of the other factor.

Module 8 · Non-parametric tests

Name the non-parametric alternatives to the independent t-test, paired t-test and one-way ANOVA.

Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon signed-rank and Kruskal–Wallis.

A Kruskal–Wallis test is significant. What do you conclude and do next?

At least one group differs. Run post hoc pairwise comparisons to find which.

Why choose a t-test over Mann–Whitney when assumptions hold?

It is more powerful: it uses the actual values, while ranks lose information.
