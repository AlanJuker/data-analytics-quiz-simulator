2. How to answer

Multiple choice

  1. Find the design first. One sample against a benchmark? Two independent groups? The same people twice? Three or more groups? Two factors? That decides the test (§7).
  2. Check the data type. Skewed, small sample, outliers or ordinal (1–5 ratings) → think non-parametric.
  3. For an output, read in this order: the assumption check (Levene's), then the p-value against .05, then the direction (means or mean ranks), then what to do next (post hoc?).
  4. Watch absolute words. "Proves", "accept H₀" and "all groups differ" are almost always wrong.

Short answer: 2–3 sentences

  1. Answer directly: name the test, the distribution or the decision.
  2. Explain why: the condition, definition or numbers that justify it.
  3. Conclude in plain language: what it means in the scenario, plus one caution or next step (post hoc test, no causation without a control group).

Example: "The same 15 employees rate their stress before, during and after a project, and the ratings are skewed. Which test, and why not Kruskal–Wallis?"

Use the Friedman test, the non-parametric test for three or more related measurements: the same employees are measured three times, and the ratings are skewed and ordinal. Kruskal–Wallis is not appropriate because it requires independent groups, with each person in only one group.

Output template: "Because p = ___ is below/above .05, we reject / fail to reject H₀. This means ___ (in the scenario's words). Next step or caution: ___."

3. Module 5 · Probability for decision making

Probability basics

  • A probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
  • To compute it, first find the total number of possible outcomes, then how many of them count as the event.
  • The Last Banana: with two dice and "highest number counts", Player 1 (1–4) wins only if both dice are 1–4 (16 of 36), so Player 2 (5–6) wins more often (20 of 36). One high die is enough.

The three distributions

BinomialPoissonNormal
ModelsNumber of successes in a fixed number of trialsNumber of events in a fixed interval of time or spaceA continuous variable that clusters around the mean
DataDiscrete count, 0 to nDiscrete count, 0, 1, 2 … with no upper limitContinuous
Parametersn (trials), p (probability of success)λ (lambda) = average number of events per intervalμ (mean) and σ (standard deviation)
ConditionsFixed n · only two outcomes · constant p · independent trialsCounts · independent events · constant rate · two events at exactly the same time is negligibleSymmetric bell · mean = median = mode · tails never touch the axis (asymptotic)
ExampleHow many of 20 customers click an adCalls per hour, arrivals per night, defects per batchHeights, delivery times, sample means
CentreMean = n × p, SD = √(np(1 − p))Mean = λ. Rescale λ to the interval (30 per hour = 5 per 10 minutes)Mean = μ

Quick way to pick: "how many out of n" → binomial · "how many per hour / per page / per km" → Poisson · "how long, how heavy, how much" (measured) → normal.

Normal distribution

  • 68–95–99.7 rule: about 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2 SD and 99.7% within 3 SD. With mean 30 and SD 5, 95% lie between 20 and 40.
  • A larger σ makes the curve wider and flatter; the mean only moves its centre.
  • z-score = (X − μ) / σ = how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. Negative = below the mean. Z-scores let you compare scores from different scales.
  • Z-table (area to the left of z): less than z = the table value · greater than z = 1 − table value · between two z's = larger value − smaller value. Example: P(Z < 1) = .8413, so P(Z > 1) = .1587.
  • The Z-table is used for confidence intervals and hypothesis testing.

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

As the sample size grows, the distribution of sample means becomes approximately normal, whatever the shape of the population. Its mean is μ and its spread (standard error) is σ / √n, so bigger samples give more precise means. This is why normal-based tests work on large samples (rule of thumb n > 30). It does not rescue small samples from skewed populations.

When the normal distribution fails, and the fix

LimitationExampleWhat to do
Assumes symmetryIncomes, spending (mean ≫ median)Log or square-root transformation
Needs continuous dataCounts of defects or callsPoisson or binomial instead
Sensitive to outliersA few huge purchasesTrim or treat the outliers, or use a non-parametric test
Underestimates extreme (tail) eventsStock-market crashes, insurance claimsAnother distribution (log-normal, exponential, Weibull)
Data have a finite rangePercentages from 0 to 100Transformation or a bounded model

4. Module 6 · Hypothesis testing

The two hypotheses

  • H₀ (null): no difference, no relationship, no effect. It is the baseline the results are measured against, e.g. μ₁ = μ₂ (hypothesized difference = 0).
  • Ha (alternative): there is a difference or relationship that is not due to chance. It is what the researcher hopes to support.
  • We either reject H₀ or fail to reject H₀. Rejecting H₀ supports Ha but does not prove it. Failing to reject does not prove H₀.

One-tailed or two-tailed: decide before collecting data

HaTestWhere α goesCritical z at α = .05Drug claims a 20% gain; the researcher thinks…
μ ≠ 20%Two-tailedSplit: 2.5% in each tail±1.96…it is different, in either direction
μ < 20%Left-tailedAll 5% in the left tail−1.645…it is lower than claimed
μ > 20%Right-tailedAll 5% in the right tail+1.645…it is higher than claimed

A direction predicted before seeing the data → one-tailed. No direction, or "is it different?" → two-tailed. In a t-table, pick the column for one- or two-tailed α and the row for the degrees of freedom.

Two ways to decide

  • p-value approach: the p-value is the probability of results at least this extreme if H₀ were true. If p ≤ α (usually .05), reject H₀. It also shows how strong the evidence is: the smaller the p, the stronger.
  • Critical value approach: if the test statistic falls beyond the critical value (in the rejection region), reject H₀. It only says inside or outside.
  • Both usually agree. Test statistic (z, t, F) = one number calculated from the sample; p-value = a probability calculated from that statistic.

z-test or t-test?

z-testt-test
Population σ knownσ unknown, estimated from the sample
Large sample (n > 30), even if σ is unknownSmall sample (n < 30)
Uses the standard normal distributionUses the t-distribution: more spread out with heavier tails, shaped by the degrees of freedom

With large samples the t-distribution is practically normal, so both tests give very similar results; software usually runs a t-test.

Errors and power

DecisionH₀ is trueH₀ is false
Reject H₀Type I error (false positive), probability αCorrect (power = 1 − β)
Fail to reject H₀CorrectType II error (false negative), probability β
  • Type I: concluding a useless drug works. Type II: concluding an effective drug does not work.
  • Raising α (.05 → .10): easier to reject H₀ → more Type I errors, fewer Type II errors, more power. Lowering α does the opposite.
  • Security guard: a guard quick to press the alarm (high α) catches more burglars but is fooled by more raccoons.
  • To gain power without more false positives, increase the sample size (or the effect size).

The three t-tests from the class activity

TestDesignActivity exampleH₀
One-sample t-testOne sample mean vs a known valueIs average salary different from the $75,000 benchmark?μ = 75,000
Independent-samples t-testTwo separate groupsDoes average salary differ between men and women?μ₁ = μ₂
Paired-samples t-testThe same people measured twice (or matched pairs)Did each employee's sales change after training? (matched by EmployeeID)Mean difference = 0
  • Independent samples come from separate populations, or from one group randomly split in two; one sample does not influence the other.
  • Levene's test decides the row of an independent t-test: p > .05 → "equal variances assumed"; p ≤ .05 → "equal variances not assumed" (Welch's t-test).
  • Confidence interval of the difference: if it excludes 0, the difference is significant; if it includes 0, it is not.
  • Does a significant paired test prove the training worked? No. It shows sales changed; without a control group, other factors (season, market) could explain it.

Steps of a test, and the report

  1. Define H₀ and Ha → select the test → choose α (.05, .01 or .10) → collect the data.
  2. Compute the test statistic → find the p-value → decide (p ≤ α: reject H₀).
  3. Report the test statistic, the p-value and the decision, then the conclusion in plain language.

Sampling strategies mentioned in this module: simple random, stratified (sample randomly within each subgroup) and cluster sampling.

5. Module 7 · ANOVA (analysis of variance)

What it is for

  • Tests whether the means of three or more independent groups differ: one categorical factor, one continuous outcome.
  • Why not several t-tests? Each test has a 5% false-positive risk, so many tests inflate the Type I error. One ANOVA keeps it at α.
  • H₀: all group means are equal. H₁: at least one mean differs.

The F-ratio

F = between-group variance ÷ within-group variance.

  • Between-group: how far the group means are from the overall mean (the effect of the factor).
  • Within-group (error variance): the spread of scores inside each group, which the factor does not explain.
  • A large F means the group means differ more than chance alone would explain.

Assumptions

AssumptionMeaningHow to checkIf violated
IndependenceOne observation does not influence anotherStudy design (random assignment)Fix the design
NormalityOf the residuals (score − its group mean)Q-Q plot, Shapiro–Wilk, Kolmogorov–SmirnovTransformation, or Kruskal–Wallis
Equal variancesGroups have similar spreadLevene's test (p > .05 = OK)Welch's ANOVA (the course's choice)
Random samplingSamples represent the populationHow the data were collectedResults cannot be generalised

Ignoring unequal variances can increase Type I errors. In a two-way design with unequal variances and unequal group sizes the results can be biased, and Welch's ANOVA is not available: use a transformation, a generalised linear model or a non-parametric alternative.

One-way vs two-way

  • One-way: one factor with 3+ levels. Example: flashcards vs summarising vs self-testing → exam score.
  • Two-way: two factors. It gives a main effect for each factor (its own effect, averaged over the other) and an interaction: the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. Example: teaching method works in classroom X but not in Y.
  • MANOVA: several dependent variables at once.

After a significant F

  • The F-test only says at least one mean differs. Run a post hoc test (Tukey HSD with equal variances; the course mentions Bonferroni when they are not) to find which pairs differ.
  • Effect size η² (eta-squared): the share of variance in the outcome explained by the factor. η² = .17 → the factor explains about 17%.

Reading an ANOVA output

Teaching method         5.84    .004   → main effect: method matters

6. Module 8 · Non-parametric tests

Parametric vs non-parametric

Parametric (t-test, ANOVA)Non-parametric
Assumes a population shape, usually normalNo specific distribution, no normality needed
Uses the raw values (means, variances)Uses ranks (or medians, signs)
More powerful when its assumptions hold, because it uses all the informationRobust when the data are skewed, small, ordinal or have outliers. An outlier just becomes the top rank.

The four tests

TestReplacesDesignH₀Example
Mann–Whitney UIndependent t-test2 independent groupsThe two distributions are the sameSatisfaction (1–5): online vs in-store customers
Wilcoxon signed-rankPaired t-test2 related measurementsMedian difference = 0Same patients' pain before and after treatment
Kruskal–WallisOne-way ANOVA3+ independent groupsAll distributions are the sameSatisfaction at three store locations
FriedmanRepeated-measures ANOVA3+ related measurementsNo difference across the measurementsSame patients at three time points

What to remember about each

  • Mann–Whitney U: ranks all values from both groups together and checks whether one group tends to have higher ranks. Assumes independent groups, ordinal or continuous data, similar shapes (only then is it a difference in medians) and no major ties. Not for paired data (→ Wilcoxon) or 3+ groups (→ Kruskal–Wallis).
  • Wilcoxon signed-rank: takes the absolute differences, ranks them, re-applies the + / − signs and tests whether the median difference is 0. Assumes pairs, differences that are at least ordinal and roughly symmetric. Choose it over a paired t-test for a small sample (n < 30) with skewed differences, Likert data or extreme outliers. With n ≥ 30 or roughly normal differences, the paired t-test is better.
  • Kruskal–Wallis: ranks all observations across groups. Hypotheses are always non-directional (no single direction exists across 3+ groups). Each person is in only one group. Check similar shapes with boxplots + Levene's test (and skewness within ±1 of each other). Similar shapes → you can talk about medians; different shapes → overall distributions. Significant → at least one group differs, so run post hoc pairwise tests.
  • All four need data that can be ranked (ordinal or continuous).
  • Two factors, non-parametric: Scheirer–Ray–Hare (extends Kruskal–Wallis to two-way designs) or ART ANOVA (aligned rank transform, the most accurate for testing interactions).

Reading the outputs

  • Mean ranks give the direction: the group with the higher mean rank tends to have higher values.
  • Wilcoxon: mostly negative ranks (after < before) with p < .05 → scores dropped significantly.
  • The sign of Z does not make a test invalid; the p-value decides.

11. Last-day recall check

Answer out loud before opening each one.

What are the four conditions of a binomial experiment?

A fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, a constant probability of success and independent trials.

How is Poisson different from binomial?

Binomial counts successes in a fixed number of trials (maximum n). Poisson counts events in a fixed interval of time or space, using the average rate λ, with no upper limit.

What does the Central Limit Theorem say, and why does it matter?

Sample means become approximately normal as n grows, whatever the population's shape, with spread σ/√n. It lets us use normal-based tests on large samples.

What does z = −1.5 mean?

The value is 1.5 standard deviations below the mean.

What is a p-value, and when do you reject H₀?

The probability of results at least this extreme if H₀ were true. Reject H₀ when p ≤ α (usually .05).

Type I vs Type II error?

Type I: rejecting a true H₀ (false positive, probability α). Type II: failing to reject a false H₀ (false negative, probability β).

What happens when α goes from .05 to .10?

H₀ is rejected more easily: more Type I errors, fewer Type II errors, more power.

When do you use a z-test instead of a t-test?

When σ is known, or the sample is large (n > 30). Otherwise a t-test.

Levene's p = .006 in an independent t-test. Which row?

Equal variances not assumed (Welch's t-test), because p ≤ .05 means the variances differ.

Why ANOVA instead of several t-tests?

Several t-tests inflate the Type I error rate; one ANOVA compares all the means in a single test at α.

What does the F-ratio compare?

Between-group variance to within-group (error) variance. A large F means the means differ more than chance explains.

What is an interaction effect?

The effect of one factor depends on the level of the other factor.

Name the non-parametric alternatives to the independent t-test, paired t-test and one-way ANOVA.

Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon signed-rank and Kruskal–Wallis.

A Kruskal–Wallis test is significant. What do you conclude and do next?

At least one group differs. Run post hoc pairwise comparisons to find which.

Why choose a t-test over Mann–Whitney when assumptions hold?

It is more powerful: it uses the actual values, while ranks lose information.
